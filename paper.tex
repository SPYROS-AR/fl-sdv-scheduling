\begin{abstract}
Training machine learning models across a fleet of Software-Defined Vehicles (SDVs) using Federated Learning (FL) means juggling three competing pressures at once: limited wireless uplink capacity, diverging client models, and the need to share onboard processors with safety-critical driving tasks. Most existing vehicular FL systems sidestep this tension by fixing the number of local training epochs and assuming dedicated compute---an approach that routinely floods the cellular uplink and starves real-time services of CPU cycles. We present a lightweight runtime policy engine that decides, on the fly, how many local epochs each vehicle should run and when it should upload, drawing on three signals: available compute slack, instantaneous link quality, and a short-horizon prediction of local learning progress. Each vehicle cycles through three states---\texttt{WAIT} (yield resources), \texttt{CONTINUE} (keep training locally), and \texttt{SYNC} (upload)---guided by a bounded exponential curve fit that forecasts diminishing returns before they materialise. We benchmark the engine on Dirichlet non-IID partitions ($\alpha_{\text{Dir}} \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$) with realistic V2X channel traces against fixed-epoch baselines ($E \in \{1, 5, 10\}$) and FedProx over 250 rounds. Under heavy data skew ($\alpha_{\text{Dir}} = 0.1$), Dynamic FedProx reaches 72.02\% centralized test accuracy---on par with the best static baseline---while cutting uplink traffic by 28\% (saving over 6.5~GB of wireless transfers), eliminating 76\% of transmission buffer overflows (from 567 down to ${\sim}138$), and reducing compute interference with safety-critical vehicle tasks by up to 74\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern Software-Defined Vehicles (SDVs) are, in effect, mobile data centres on wheels. They run sophisticated perception, planning, and diagnostics workloads while continuously producing large volumes of high-dimensional sensor data. Federated Learning (FL) is a natural fit for this setting: it lets an entire fleet collaboratively improve a shared model without ever uploading raw, privacy-sensitive telemetry to the cloud.

Bringing FL into a vehicular network, however, exposes two operational challenges that set it apart from conventional edge deployments:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Volatile V2X links.} Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) radio channels suffer from time-varying fading, handovers, and limited cell-sector uplink capacity. Pushing large parameter updates from every vehicle at every round quickly saturates the available uplink. Reducing upload frequency helps the channel but, on non-IID vehicular data, causes local models to drift apart and degrades the global aggregate.
    \item \textbf{Mixed-criticality compute sharing.} SDVs consolidate FL training on the same hardware that runs safety-critical ECU services---ADAS perception, object tracking, trajectory planning. FL is strictly a background task: it must yield processor time whenever a higher-priority workload needs it. A rigid, fixed-epoch training schedule that ignores real-time CPU occupancy risks starving safety tasks and missing hard deadlines.
\end{enumerate}

Existing vehicular FL schedulers typically optimise link-level resources---transmit power, rate adaptation, client selection---while treating onboard compute as either unlimited or statically partitioned. Two gaps stand out. First, no current framework asks whether the training it schedules actually produces useful model improvement; under non-IID data a vehicle may hit diminishing returns after just two local epochs, yet a fixed-$E$ policy forces it to keep going---wasting cycles, inflating drift, and ultimately hurting global convergence. Second, existing formulations ignore the time-varying nature of safety-workload demand: when an ADAS pipeline spikes, a scheduler that blindly continues training can cause deadline violations in safety-critical tasks.

We address both gaps with an online policy engine that coordinates compute allocation, training depth, and upload timing through a single utility-maximisation objective:
\begin{equation*}
\max \sum \left[ \delta \cdot G_i(t) - \alpha C_{\text{net}}(t) - \beta C_{\text{comp}}(t) - \gamma C_{\text{drift}}(t) \right]
\end{equation*}
Rather than assuming dedicated hardware, the engine monitors co-located workload occupancy, channel capacity, and model divergence to transition each vehicle among three execution states: \texttt{WAIT} (idle---yield compute to safety tasks), \texttt{CONTINUE} (keep training locally while persisting weights on disk), and \texttt{SYNC} (upload the parameter update). The explicit \texttt{WAIT} state is especially important: it stops vehicles from burning energy or contending with ADAS tasks when the channel is poor and local training would yield negligible improvement.

In summary, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A formulation of the vehicular FL scheduling problem as an online utility-maximisation framework that balances empirical learning gain against uplink bandwidth cost, safety-critical compute contention, and parameter drift.
    \item A three-state runtime policy engine (\texttt{WAIT}, \texttt{CONTINUE}, \texttt{SYNC}) that uses bounded non-linear exponential curve fitting to predict marginal validation gain before committing to additional local epochs.
    \item An empirical evaluation on non-IID Dirichlet partitions ($\alpha_{\text{Dir}} \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$) against fixed-epoch baselines ($E \in \{1, 5, 10\}$) and FedProx over 250 rounds, showing a 28\% reduction in uplink traffic, a 76\% drop in transmission buffer overflows (from 567 to ${\sim}$138), and up to 74\% lower compute contention with safety-critical tasks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Prior work on vehicular federated learning falls broadly into three categories: mobility-aware transmission scheduling, joint compute--communication optimisation, and compressed or asynchronous transfer schemes.

\subsection{Mobility-Aware Transmission Scheduling}
A number of studies tackle the challenge of uploading models over unreliable, time-varying wireless links. Hong et al.~\cite{ref_hong} cast V2I model uploads as a Markov Decision Process to jointly minimise latency and energy. Karatas et al.~\cite{ref_karatas} introduced variable-rate client selection that accounts for imperfect channel state information, while Yan et al.~\cite{ref_yan} exploited opportunistic V2V relays to boost upload success rates under energy constraints. Although these methods handle link degradation effectively, they treat vehicular compute as an unconstrained or static resource.

\subsection{Joint Compute and Communication Co-Design}
Several frameworks explicitly couple onboard processing time with wireless transmission delay. Qiang et al.~\cite{ref_qiang} partitioned model layers dynamically across vehicular edge nodes according to real-time processor and bandwidth availability. Ballotta et al.~\cite{ref_ballotta} used radio environment maps to schedule computation predictively. Zhang et al.~\cite{ref_zhang_selection} jointly optimised client selection and resource allocation for convergence accuracy, and Pervej et al.~\cite{ref_pervej} folded CPU frequencies and iteration counts into physical resource block allocation. Li et al.~\cite{ref_fedprox} proposed FedProx, adding a proximal regularisation term that mitigates system and statistical heterogeneity. While these methods link computation latency to transmission budgets, they generally assume a dedicated hardware partition for FL.

\subsection{Compressed and Asynchronous Frameworks}
Quantisation and asynchronous aggregation offer complementary ways to ease communication bottlenecks. Liu et al.~\cite{ref_liu} tuned batch sizes and gradient compression ratios under latency bounds; Zhang et al.~\cite{ref_zhang_quant} adapted quantisation levels to channel conditions. On the asynchronous side, Wu et al.~\cite{ref_wu} scaled upload frequency to instantaneous link quality, and Zhang et al.~\cite{ref_zhang_automotive} demonstrated sliding-window parameter transfers on embedded automotive GPUs.

Table~\ref{tab:related_work} maps each prior approach against its compute model, network model, and optimisation target. A clear pattern emerges: existing methods optimise radio resource blocks or static computation partitions, but none of them jointly ask two questions that matter in practice: (i)~will one more local epoch actually produce a measurable improvement? and (ii)~can the vehicle's processor afford that epoch without starving a co-located safety workload? Our engine fills both gaps by introducing a dynamic compute-occupancy metric ($U_i^{\text{crit}}(t)$), a bounded exponential gain predictor, and an online heuristic that co-optimises training depth and communication timing in real time.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
We consider $N$ vehicles, indexed $\mathcal{V} = \{1, \dots, N\}$, that communicate with an edge or roadside FL server over discrete decision rounds $t \in \{0, 1, \dots, T{-}1\}$.

\subsection{Vehicle State Representation}
At each round $t$, vehicle $i$ observes a local state vector:
\begin{equation}
S_i(t) = \{B_i(t),\, U_i^{\text{crit}}(t),\, D_i(t),\, G_i^{\text{prior}}(t)\}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $B_i(t)$ represents the instantaneous uplink bandwidth available to vehicle $i$ (in Mbps).
    \item $U_i^{\text{crit}}(t) \in [0, 1]$ denotes the fraction of onboard compute capacity occupied by safety-critical tasks (e.g., ADAS perception and trajectory planning).
    \item $D_i(t)$ indicates client model drift relative to the latest global model parameters.
    \item $G_i^{\text{prior}}(t)$ denotes the historical empirical learning gain achieved during the vehicle's preceding active training window.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cost and Gain Components}
Training round $t$ is governed by four operational metrics.

\subsubsection{Communication Cost}
Let $p_i(t) \in \{0,1\}$ indicate whether vehicle $i$ uploads its model at round $t$. The network cost is:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{net}}(t) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}} p_i(t) \frac{M_i}{B_i(t)}
\end{equation}
where $M_i$ is the parameter payload size in megabytes (e.g., 9.31~MiB for an FP32 ResNet-9).

\subsubsection{Compute Contention Cost}
Let $c_i(t) = e_i(t)/\rho_i \in [0,1]$ denote the fraction of compute capacity consumed by FL, where $e_i(t)$ is the number of executed local epochs and $\rho_i$ the maximum epoch budget. The interference imposed on co-located safety tasks is:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{comp}}(t) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}} c_i(t) U_i^{\text{crit}}(t)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Parameter Divergence (Model Drift)}
We measure how far each vehicle's local parameters $\mathbf{w}_{\text{local},i}(t)$ have strayed from the global model $\mathbf{w}_{\text{global}}(t)$ using a straightforward Euclidean distance:
\begin{equation}
D_i(t) = \| \mathbf{w}_{\text{local}, i}(t) - \mathbf{w}_{\text{global}}(t) \|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{l=1}^L \| \mathbf{w}_{i, l}(t) - \mathbf{w}_{\text{global}, l}(t) \|_2^2}
\end{equation}
where $l \in \{1, \dots, L\}$ indexes weight and bias tensors. Summing over the fleet gives the cumulative drift:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{drift}}(t) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}} D_i(t)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Learning Gain and Marginal Utility}
We track how much local training actually reduces validation loss:
\begin{equation}
G_i(t) = \max\left(0,\, L_i(\mathbf{w}_{\text{global}}(t)) - L_i(\mathbf{w}_{\text{local}, i}(t))\right)
\end{equation}
At local epoch $e$, the vehicle asks whether running one more epoch is worthwhile by evaluating a marginal utility score:
\begin{equation}
\Delta U_i(e+1) = \delta \cdot \widehat{G}_i(e+1) - \beta \frac{U_i^{\text{crit}}(t)}{\rho_i} - \gamma D_i(e+1)
\label{eq:marginal_utility}
\end{equation}
Here $\widehat{G}_i(e{+}1)$ is the predicted loss reduction from curve fitting, and $\delta, \beta, \gamma > 0$ are weighting coefficients.

This per-epoch check lets each vehicle adapt its training depth on the fly, avoiding two pathologies of fixed-epoch FL: (i)~\emph{under-training}, where data-rich clients are artificially capped, and (ii)~\emph{over-training}, where clients with depleted or redundant data burn compute and pile up destructive drift.

\subsection{Global Fleet Objective}
Across the full time horizon $T$ the fleet seeks to maximise:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\{c_i(t), e_i(t), p_i(t)\}} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \left[ \delta \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}} G_i(t) - \alpha C_{\text{net}}(t) - \beta C_{\text{comp}}(t) - \gamma C_{\text{drift}}(t) \right]
\label{eq:global_opt}
\end{equation}

The positive term rewards measurable learning progress; the three penalty terms discourage bandwidth waste, safety-task interference, and parameter divergence. This structure prevents the trivially cheap solution (never train) from dominating---since it forfeits all learning reward---and equally prevents the trivially greedy solution (train without limit) from dominating, since it racks up resource costs. The policy engine steers towards the productive middle ground.

\noindent subject to:
\begin{align}
& c_i(t) \le 1 - U_i^{\text{crit}}(t), \quad \forall i, t \label{eq:slack_constraint} \\
& e_i(t) \le \rho_i c_i(t), \quad \forall i, t \label{eq:epochs_constraint} \\
& \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}} p_i(t) M_i \le B_{\text{cell}}(t), \quad \forall t \label{eq:cell_constraint} \\
& D_i(t) \le D_i^{\text{max}}, \quad \forall i, t \label{eq:drift_constraint}
\end{align}

Constraint~\eqref{eq:slack_constraint} keeps FL within the available compute slack. Constraint~\eqref{eq:epochs_constraint} bounds local epochs by that slack. Constraint~\eqref{eq:cell_constraint} caps simultaneous uploads to the cell's total uplink capacity $B_{\text{cell}}(t)$, and~\eqref{eq:drift_constraint} prevents any single client from drifting too far.

Because future channel quality and safety-workload spikes are fundamentally unpredictable, solving~\eqref{eq:global_opt} offline is intractable. We therefore implement it as an online, deterministic policy engine that approximates the objective at each decision interval.

\section{Runtime Policy Engine}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Dynamic Runtime Policy Engine for FL Vehicle $i$}
\label{alg:policy_engine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE State $S_i(t)$, local model $\mathbf{w}_i$, thresholds $\{B_{\text{thresh}}, G_{\text{thresh}}, \tau_{\text{continue}}, D_i^{\text{max}}\}$, compute ceiling $\rho_i$, weights $\{\delta, \beta, \gamma\}$.
\STATE $c_i^{\text{max}} \leftarrow \max(0, 1 - U_i^{\text{crit}}(t))$
\IF{$c_i^{\text{max}} < 1 / \rho_i$}
    \RETURN \texttt{WAIT} ($e_i \leftarrow 0, p_i \leftarrow 0$) \COMMENT{Safety workload starvation}
\ENDIF
\IF{$B_i(t) < B_{\text{thresh}}$ \AND $G_i^{\text{prior}} \le G_{\text{thresh}}$}
    \RETURN \texttt{WAIT} ($e_i \leftarrow 0, p_i \leftarrow 0$) \COMMENT{Poor channel, low utility}
\ENDIF
\STATE Initialize $e \leftarrow 0$, history $H \leftarrow [L_i(0)]$
\WHILE{$(e + 1)/\rho_i \le c_i^{\text{max}}$ \AND $e < \rho_i$}
    \STATE Execute 1 local epoch on $\mathbf{w}_i$
    \STATE $e \leftarrow e + 1$; measure $L_i(e)$
    \STATE Smooth: $L_{\text{smooth}} \leftarrow \theta L_i(e) + (1-\theta) H[\text{last}]$; append to $H$
    \IF{$e < 2$}
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE Extrapolate $\widehat{L}(e+1) \leftarrow \text{BoundedExpFit}(H)$
    \STATE $\widehat{G} \leftarrow \max(0, H[\text{last}] - \widehat{L}(e+1))$
    \STATE $D_i \leftarrow \| \mathbf{w}_i - \mathbf{w}_{\text{global}} \|_2$
    \STATE $\Delta U \leftarrow \delta \widehat{G} - \beta (U_i^{\text{crit}} / \rho_i) - \gamma D_i$
    \IF{$D_i > D_i^{\text{max}}$ \OR $\widehat{G} < G_{\text{thresh}}$ \OR $\Delta U < \tau_{\text{continue}}$}
        \STATE \textbf{break}
    \ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\STATE $G_i^{\text{actual}} \leftarrow \max(0, L_i(0) - L_i(e))$; $G_i^{\text{prior}} \leftarrow G_i^{\text{actual}}$
\IF{$e = 0$ \OR ($B_i(t) < B_{\text{thresh}}$ \AND $G_i^{\text{actual}} \le G_{\text{thresh}}$)}
    \RETURN \texttt{WAIT} ($e_i \leftarrow 0, p_i \leftarrow 0$)
\ENDIF
\IF{$B_i(t) \ge B_{\text{thresh}}$}
    \RETURN \texttt{SYNC} ($e_i \leftarrow e, p_i \leftarrow 1$) \COMMENT{Upload model update}
\ELSE
    \RETURN \texttt{CONTINUE} ($e_i \leftarrow e, p_i \leftarrow 0$) \COMMENT{Retain weights locally}
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

At each round, every vehicle evaluates its local constraints and transitions into one of three states (see Algorithm~\ref{alg:policy_engine}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{WAIT (Idle).} The vehicle runs zero local epochs and sends nothing. This happens when (i) safety tasks leave too little CPU headroom for even a single epoch ($1 - U_i^{\text{crit}} < 1/\rho_i$), or (ii) the uplink is degraded ($B_i < B_{\text{thresh}}$) and prior learning progress was negligible ($G_i^{\text{prior}} \le G_{\text{thresh}}$). Entering \texttt{WAIT} prevents wasted energy and avoids accumulating drift when neither transmission nor meaningful training is feasible.
    \item \textbf{CONTINUE (Local Training).} When compute slack permits training but the uplink is congested, yet local loss is still actively decreasing, the vehicle keeps training without uploading. Crucially, its weights are persisted across rounds rather than reset to the global model, enabling multi-round autonomous optimisation.
    \item \textbf{SYNC (Upload).} Once local training finishes and the link can support it ($B_i(t) \ge B_{\text{thresh}}$), the vehicle uploads its updated parameters for federated aggregation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Bounded Exponential Gain Prediction}
Before committing additional compute to epoch $e{+}1$, the vehicle fits an exponential decay curve to its observed validation-loss history:
\begin{equation}
L(e) \approx L_\infty + A e^{-B e}
\end{equation}
Fitting this model via unconstrained non-linear regression on short, noisy loss traces ($N \ge 3$ points) often produces nonsensical estimates---negative asymptotic losses or negative decay rates. To keep predictions well-behaved, we solve a bounded non-linear least-squares problem:
\begin{equation}
\min_{L_\infty, A, B} \sum_{k=0}^{e} \left( L(k) - (L_\infty + A e^{-B k}) \right)^2
\end{equation}
subject to $0 \le L_\infty \le \min(H)$, $A \ge 0$, and $10^{-4} \le B \le 10.0$, with initial guess $p_0 = [0.8\min(H),\; H[0] - 0.8\min(H),\; 0.5]$. If the solver fails to converge within 1\,500 iterations, the engine falls back to a simple linear-slope decay:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{L}(e+1) = \max\left(0,\, L(e) + 0.5 \cdot (L(e) - L(e-1))\right)
\end{equation}
This bounded design keeps the gain predictor stable and prevents premature early stopping.

\section{Performance Evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We implement the testbed in PyTorch with the Flower FL framework. Each vehicle trains a ResNet-9 model modified with Group Normalisation (8 groups per convolutional stage) to avoid running-statistic divergence under non-IID partitions. Training uses the CIFAR-10 dataset distributed across $N = 10$ vehicles via symmetric Dirichlet sampling ($\alpha_{\text{Dir}} \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5\}$), spanning extreme to moderate statistical heterogeneity.

We compare against the following baselines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Static FedAvg with fixed local epochs $E \in \{1, 5, 10\}$.
    \item Fixed FedProx ($E = 5$, $\mu = 0.01$) to assess the effect of proximal regularisation on client drift.
    \item Our dynamic policy in two variants---Dynamic FedAvg and Dynamic FedProx---with calibrated coefficients: $\alpha = 0.005$, $\beta = 1.0$, $\gamma = 0.006$, $\delta = 27.0$, $B_{\text{thresh}} = 20.0$~Mbps, and $G_{\text{thresh}} = 0.03$. These weights were selected from the non-dominated front of a 100-trial offline hyperparameter sweep.
\end{itemize}
Network traces and vehicular safety-workload occupancy $U_i^{\text{crit}}(t)$ are driven by empirical V2X channel data, with total cell uplink capacity modelled as a sinusoidal envelope ($B_{\text{cell}}(t) = B_{\text{max}}(0.8 + 0.2\sin(t))$) to stress-test the engine under shared infrastructure bottlenecks.

\subsection{Communication Efficiency vs.\ Global Accuracy}
To evaluate practical communication efficiency over constrained V2X links, Figures 1 and 2 measure convergence directly against cumulative network megabytes transmitted rather than abstract round indices. Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_results} and Figures~\ref{fig:accuracy_vs_mb}--\ref{fig:accuracy_vs_uplink} tell a consistent story: the dynamic engine reaches accuracy on par with the best fixed baselines while spending far less airtime.

The root cause of waste in static FL is the V2I uplink bottleneck. Base stations can broadcast global models on wide downlink channels, but vehicle uploads share a narrow, fading uplink. Fixed schedulers force every sampled vehicle to attempt an upload each round regardless of channel conditions. Over 250 rounds, all static baselines accumulate 23\,272~MB (${\sim}$23.3~GB) of raw uplink traffic and trigger 567 buffer overflows when vehicles try to push through congested channels.

Our engine, by contrast, monitors both channel quality and expected learning progress before permitting transmission. When the link deteriorates, vehicles transition to \texttt{CONTINUE} or \texttt{WAIT}, keeping cumulative uplink transfers to roughly 16\,700~MB---a 28\% reduction that saves over 6.5~GB of wireless transfers---while buffer overflows drop by 76\% (from 567 to ${\sim}$138).

These savings come at negligible accuracy cost. Under extreme heterogeneity ($\alpha_{\text{Dir}} = 0.1$), Dynamic FedProx reaches 72.02\%, closely matching the best static baseline (Fixed FedAvg $E{=}5$ at 72.51\%) and comfortably outperforming Fixed $E{=}1$ (68.98\%). Dynamic FedAvg achieves 70.18\%, comparable to Fixed FedProx (71.13\%) while using 28\% less uplink bandwidth. Under moderate and mild skew ($\alpha \in \{0.3, 0.5\}$) all methods converge to a similar accuracy band (${\sim}$76.8--77.8\% and ${\sim}$78.1--79.3\%), yet the dynamic engine reaches this parity while transmitting roughly a quarter less data and virtually eliminating upload failures.

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy_vs_time} plots accuracy against wall-clock time. By sidestepping congested transmission windows and deferring training during peak safety-workload periods, the dynamic engine avoids straggler delays and maintains steady progress.

\subsection{Compute Contention and Model Drift}
Figure~\ref{fig:compute_interference} compares the cumulative compute interference each policy imposes on safety-critical vehicle tasks. Static schedules pay no attention to co-located processor occupancy: Fixed $E{=}10$ racks up 1\,000.6 units of contention, and Fixed $E{=}5$ reaches 500.3, both repeatedly training through periods when ADAS workloads leave minimal spare cycles. Our engine enforces an active slack threshold ($\text{slack} \ge 2/\rho_i$): whenever the safety workload exceeds ${\sim}80$\% of processor capacity, training yields to higher-priority tasks. As a result, the dynamic policies keep cumulative contention between 261.9 and 385.2 across all non-IID settings---up to 74\% lower than Fixed $E{=}10$ and 47\% lower than Fixed $E{=}5$.

Figure~\ref{fig:model_drift} shows parameter drift measured as the Euclidean distance between local and global weights. High-epoch static training allows local models to diverge sharply under non-IID skew (Fixed $E{=}10$ accumulates 4\,474.1 in cumulative drift at $\alpha{=}0.1$), leading to destructive interference during aggregation. Dynamic FedProx curtails this to 2\,724.2 at $\alpha{=}0.1$ and 2\,149.4 at $\alpha{=}0.5$---a 39\% reduction compared to Fixed $E{=}10$---by combining bounded utility checks that truncate unpromising epochs with proximal regularisation that anchors updates to the global model.

Figure~\ref{fig:utility} tracks cumulative system utility across all three Dirichlet settings. Despite transmitting less and training more selectively, the dynamic engine accumulates utility at a rate that keeps pace with (and in many settings matches) the best static baselines, confirming that the operational savings are not bought at the expense of learning progress.

\subsection{Client State Dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:client_states} shows how vehicles distribute their time across the three execution states. Static schemes operate in a rigid transmit-or-fail pattern: every sampled client attempts an upload regardless of conditions, producing 1\,933 upload attempts and 567 buffer overflows over 250 rounds.

The dynamic engine paints a different picture. Under severe skew ($\alpha_{\text{Dir}} = 0.1$), Dynamic FedAvg vehicles entered \texttt{CONTINUE} 474 times---intervals where channel throughput was too low for a safe upload but continued local training still promised meaningful loss reduction. Because weights persist on disk across rounds, these vehicles kept refining their models rather than throwing away progress. Another 230 transitions went to \texttt{WAIT}, representing moments when ADAS tasks needed the processor or when both the channel and the expected learning gain were poor. Buffer overflows consequently fell from 567 to 136---a 76\% reduction---transforming wasted network collisions into either productive local training or deliberate, protected idling.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented an online runtime policy engine for federated learning in Software-Defined Vehicles that tackles the tension between mixed-criticality onboard compute demands and time-varying V2X wireless capacity. By steering each vehicle through three execution states---\texttt{WAIT}, \texttt{CONTINUE}, and \texttt{SYNC}---using bounded loss extrapolation and real-time slack inspection, the engine ensures that learning neither starves safety-critical workloads nor overwhelms the cellular uplink. On non-IID Dirichlet-partitioned benchmarks the dynamic engine matches the convergence accuracy of the best static baselines while reducing uplink traffic by 28\%, cutting buffer overflows by 76\%, and lowering compute interference on safety tasks by up to 74\%. Future work will integrate vehicle trajectory prediction to anticipate cellular handovers and investigate 5G NR sidelink (V2V) cooperative parameter exchanges for distributed local aggregation.
